# The eight storytelling lenses

## Source and interpretation

Source: a supplied transcript titled **The Storytelling Genius Of Steve Jobs**. The source materials did not include a verified creator name, video URL, or reliable timestamps. The original transcript is not included in this repository. The transcript includes narration and excerpts of presentations. The eight names and their order below come from the narrator's analysis; they are not a framework Jobs explicitly published.

The source anchors below identify examples within that transcript. The review questions, editing moves, and cautions are applications developed for demo-script review. They are not quotations or additional claims made by the video. Unrelated contemporary claims, personality judgments, and historical claims are not needed for the skill and are not independently verified here.

## 1. Analogy

**Transcript anchor:** Early Apple appearances; the bicycle comparison presents a computer as a tool that amplifies human ability.

**Lesson:** Connect an unfamiliar idea to a familiar experience so the audience can understand its value.

**Review for:** A useful comparison that explains the capability or benefit, rather than merely decorating the language. Would this audience recognize it? Does the comparison preserve the important limits?

**Editing move:** Replace a dense explanation with one brief, accurate comparison, then return to what the product actually does. For specialists, keep precise technical language when it communicates better.

**Watch for:** Mixed metaphors, cute but irrelevant comparisons, and metaphors that suggest autonomy, speed, completeness, or certainty the product does not have.

## 2. Concretization

**Transcript anchor:** Early interviews make the personal computer imaginable through its physical portability rather than enclosure specifications.

**Lesson:** Give the audience a tangible experience they can picture, instead of asking them to interpret an abstraction.

**Review for:** Vague claims such as seamless experiences or transformative productivity without a person, task, object, action, or observable change.

**Editing move:** Replace the abstraction with a supported detail: which person is trying to do what, what they interact with, and what changes. Use the script's actual scenario. If a new scenario would help, label it illustrative. Analogy compares one thing to another; concretization makes this thing specific. Either can work without the other.

**Watch for:** Invented customer stories or fabricated numbers used to make copy sound more concrete. A precise unsupported claim is still unsupported.

## 3. Demonstration

**Transcript anchor:** On-stage use of music players, FaceTime, and GarageBand; contrast with persuasive claims lacking a working demonstration.

**Lesson:** Let the audience observe the product doing the thing the story promises.

**Review for:** A central claim with a corresponding action and inspectable result. Does the visible evidence establish the outcome, or merely that a button was pressed, a screen changed, or a draft appeared?

**Editing move:** Connect promise → presenter action → visible evidence → audience consequence. Shorten narration that tells the audience what a clear result can show. If proof is unavailable, narrow the promise or explicitly present the intended experience as a prototype, recording, or simulation, as appropriate.

**Watch for:** Upgrading preparation into verification, passing simulated output off as live behavior, or changing planned work into a claim of completion. Stage confidence cannot repair a missing capability.

## 4. Rapport

**Transcript anchor:** The narrator describes Jobs inviting the audience to explore a product with him, including the scrolling anecdote.

**Lesson:** Build trust through a shared perspective and respectful curiosity.

**Review for:** Whether the script recognizes the audience's actual goals, experience, constraints, and skepticism. Does the presenter guide a discovery or demand admiration?

**Editing move:** Start from a recognizable audience problem; invite attention to a meaningful detail; let the audience see why it matters. Keep the language natural to this presenter. For an interactive demo, allow a relevant response or question without making an assumed answer necessary to proceed.

**Watch for:** Vendor self-congratulation, forced audience participation, talking down to experts, and assertions about what everyone must want. Rapport does not require casual language in a formal setting.

## 5. Framing

**Transcript anchor:** The Macintosh/IBM story, product and interface evolution, and the reframing of Microsoft's role during Apple's recovery.

**Lesson:** Meaningful context changes how the audience understands the need for a product or decision.

**Review for:** A credible account of the current situation, its limitation, and why the proposed change matters. A timeline is one option; a practical before-and-after contrast is often sufficient.

**Editing move:** Briefly connect the audience's existing approach to the limitation they now face and the improvement this demonstration can establish. Acknowledge what already works. Get to proof before background becomes a history lecture.

**Watch for:** Invented inevitability, unsupported competitive comparisons, dismissing existing investments, or a progression that exists only because the vendor wants to sell its next product.

## 6. Redefinition

**Transcript anchor:** Asking about the largest education company shifts the audience's category for Apple.

**Lesson:** A more useful interpretation of a familiar category can expose overlooked value.

**Review for:** A category label or assumption that prevents the audience from seeing the real problem or benefit. Is the new interpretation supported by the demonstrated behavior?

**Editing move:** Offer a clearer customer-centered interpretation, then substantiate it in the demo. For example, if the script shows handoffs and ownership, it may be more useful to discuss the experience as knowing who acts next than merely displaying a dashboard. This is a conditional illustration, not a claim about the reviewed product.

**Watch for:** Renaming a feature without changing understanding, inflated category claims, or redefining a term to sidestep a limitation. Framing supplies context; redefinition changes what the audience thinks the thing or problem is.

## 7. Soul

**Transcript anchor:** The Stanford stories about calligraphy, rejection, meaningful work, and mortality; the narrator associates them with authenticity and human meaning.

**Lesson:** A story carries weight when it connects to something people sincerely care about.

**Review for:** A believable human stake behind the capability: frustration, uncertainty, confidence, creative work, or another relevant concern supported by the scenario. Does the line sound like this presenter could mean it?

**Editing move:** Connect the observed outcome to a person and a meaningful consequence. Use real supplied anecdotes when appropriate; otherwise use a modest statement of the scenario's stakes. Label invented examples as illustrative rather than pretending they happened.

**Watch for:** Manufactured vulnerability, forced origin stories, melodrama, assumed feelings, or emotional promises beyond the evidence. Authenticity does not require private disclosure or imitation of Jobs's biography.

## 8. Showmanship

**Transcript anchor:** The iPhone's three-functions/one-device reveal, the pause around scrolling, and physical product reveals from an envelope or bag.

**Lesson:** Sequence, anticipation, contrast, humor, and pauses can help the audience notice and remember a meaningful result.

**Review for:** Whether a reveal has a clear setup and a payoff the demo can deliver. Does narration spoil or obscure the moment? Is the delivery appropriate to the format, audience, and available time?

**Editing move:** Establish a relevant question, show the supported result, pause so the audience can inspect it, then explain its significance. In a recorded demo, an edit or visual emphasis can serve the role of a stage pause. Simple discovery is often enough.

**Watch for:** Gimmicks, copied catchphrases, suspense that delays understanding, invented audience amazement, hidden preparation, or surprises that blur product truth. Never trade clarity or evidence for drama.
